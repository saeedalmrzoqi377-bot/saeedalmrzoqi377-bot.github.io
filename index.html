Three reflection answers

1

What is the main change and central argument?

The main change is the movement from pearling as a major source of income to pearling as protected cultural heritage. My timeline argues that local skill did not suddenly lose its value. Outside forces changed the system: cultured pearls created cheaper competition, the Great Depression reduced spending, and oil later offered new jobs. Today, museums and pearl farms keep the knowledge alive, even though pearling no longer supports the economy in the same way.

2

Which event is the turning point, and why?

The arrival of cultured pearls in the 1920s is the turning point. Natural Gulf pearls were rare and expensive, and divers took serious risks to collect them. Cultured pearls could be produced more regularly and sold for less. This changed what buyers expected and weakened demand for Gulf pearls. The Great Depression made the decline faster, but cultured pearls had already changed the market.

3

How do images and media strengthen the argument?

Images show the human side of the industry. The diving photographs reveal teamwork and danger. The Delma Museum shows how trade wealth became heritage, and the oil tanker shows the scale of economic change. The modern pearl farm connects the past with sustainability and tourism. With text only, the audience would know the facts but would feel less of the effort, risk, and transformation.

Research sources

  1. Abu Dhabi Culture: Marawah Island — early settlement, pearling, and maritime trade.
  2. Abu Dhabi Culture: Pearl Diving — season, crew roles, tools, and working conditions.
  3. Department of Culture and Tourism – Abu Dhabi: Delma Museum — the peak period, Delma market, trade links, decline, and Bait Al Muraikhi.
  4. UAE Interact: Economic and social history — decline during the 1930s and the move into the oil era.
  5. ADNOC: Our Heritage — early oil milestones and archival images.
  6. Suwaidi Pearls — the 2004 revival of Arabian pearl culture in Al Rams.
